How wrong the models still are

Next-word loss as they train: on the text they learn from, and on held-out text they never see. Lower is better.
What is loss?

At every word, a model gives each possible next word a chance. Loss measures how surprised it was by the word that really came next. Lower means it saw it coming.

A loss of L is as hard as picking the right word out of about eL equally likely ones: 3.3 is like choosing among 27 words.

Each model has two lines in two colors. The training line is the loss on the batches it is learning from, as they go by. The held-out line is the loss on text it never trains on (the "finish line" scores above use those). If the held-out line stays close to the training one, the model is learning things that carry over to new text; if it starts climbing away above it, the model is memorising its training text. Published models (OpenAI GPT-2, SmolLM2) are left out of this chart: they trained on other text, or maybe on these very pages, so their loss can't be compared fairly. Compare them on the tests in "The finish line".

The dashed lines are forecasts of where each line will be at the planned end, with the likely range shaded around them (about 8 times in 10 the end lands inside it). See "How the forecasts work" below.

How the forecasts work, and how good they are

What it fits. A model learns in proportion to the learning rate it gets: a word read at half the rate teaches about half as much. So the curve is fitted against learning so far (the learning rate added up over the words read), not against the words alone. The schedule is planned, so we know that number for the rest of the run too, including how it slows down in the cool-down.

The cool-down. Over the last 20% (Bee4: 30%) the learning rate falls to zero, and the loss drops by an extra amount that a plain curve can't see coming: the steps get small enough to settle into the valley instead of bouncing across it. That drop builds up over a few hundred steps after each cut. Before a run gets there, its own points can't say how big the drop will be, so its size is taken from the runs on the pod that already went through a cool-down (as a share of their loss), and the range is widened by how much that varied. Once the run is in its own cool-down, its points take over.

The text change. At 80% (Bee4: 70%) the text mix changes too (more conversations, which are easier to guess), so the training loss steps down at once; the held-out text stays the same, so the held-out loss doesn't. The training forecast has that step in it (sized the same way); the held-out one doesn't.

The noise. The training loss jumps around, so it is first smoothed (the middle value of each stretch), and its shape is borrowed from the much steadier held-out loss: same model, same learning. HellaSwag moves with the held-out loss almost in a straight line, so its forecast is the held-out forecast pushed through that line.

The range. The same method is replayed from earlier moments of the run, looking exactly as far ahead (for the share of the run still to go) as it looks now, and checked against what really happened. Their typical miss makes the range, plus the doubt about the cool-down drop. Early in a run, with little to check against, it starts wide on purpose.

100B and 1T. These buttons show the whole run again, had it been planned that long on the same recipe. Everything the recipe ties to the share of the run moves with it: the flat learning rate lasts longer, the cool-down and text change sit in its last 20%, the model grows at its 10% and 25% (so it stays small for longer) and the mentor fades by its 30%. How much a word is worth to the smaller model, and with the mentor, is learned from the real run's own early stretch along with the curve. Its training time is worked out the same way, from the speeds the real run had at each depth and with the mentor. They assume fresh text all the way and stretch a curve far past its points, so the range there is mostly how far apart the curve shapes the points can't rule out end up. The x axis turns logarithmic.

(For the curious: loss = floor + A × learning-so-far−α − C × how much the learning rate has come down, from Tissue et al., 2024, "Scaling Law with Learning Rate Annealing".)

HellaSwag

Picking the sensible ending of a scene, out of 4, scored per letter. Higher is better.
What is HellaSwag?

A standard test of common sense: "A woman is outside with a bucket and a dog. The dog is running around trying to avoid a bath. She…" followed by four endings, three of them silly.

The model picks the ending it finds least surprising. Guessing gives 25%. OpenAI's GPT-2 small scores about 31.1% this way: that is the line to beat.

Per letter. A long ending piles up more surprise than a short one just by being long, so each ending's total surprise is divided by its number of letters before the model picks. This is how published results for small models are scored (lm-evaluation-harness calls it acc_norm; the SmolLM2 numbers are this kind), and it is fair across tokenizers, because letters are the same for every model. Every HellaSwag number on this page is this score. Checks made before Nov 5 only had a score per token, so they are left out until the run is re-tested (python3 -m oazy.rescore).

During training a quick check uses 1,000 questions spread over the whole set; the final score uses all 10,042. Older runs took the first 1,000 instead, which are easier and read about 5 points too high, so those points are hidden: for such a run only the final score is drawn.

How they use what they read

Tide's drafts: how far ahead can it see?

Loss of Tide's draft of the word 1, 2, … 8 words ahead (1 = the ordinary next-word loss). Each line is one check during training.
Why does this matter?

Every Tide draft is trained to guess the real word it stands for. If the far drafts get much better than "any common word", the model is genuinely planning ahead, and those plans are what the next-word guess listens to. If they stay flat, the belt is not earning its keep.

Fern's rounds: how hard is each step of the unfolding?

Loss of the words Fern decides in each of its 5 rounds. Round 1 picks the 16th word of a passage from the past alone; round 5 fills single gaps between two known words. Each line is one check during training.
Why does this matter?

Fern writes a passage of 16 words like a fern frond unfurls: first where the passage is going (word 16), then the middle of the way (word 8), then the middle of each half (4 and 12), and so on, until the last round fills every gap. Its total loss is simply the average over all words, so it compares directly with the GPT-2's.

Early rounds are long-range guesses and should stay hardest. If the later rounds fall far below the GPT-2's loss, Fern is really using the future it has already decided, on both sides of each gap. That is the whole point of writing coarse to fine.

What memory buys

Loss when a model may only use the last few words, versus all of them.
How to read this

We cut the same texts into pieces of 4, 32, 256 words and let each model read every piece on its own, so it can only use what is inside the piece. At the right edge it reads the whole text.

A model that ignores what it read earlier gives a flat line. The steeper the drop, the more it is using the words further back. For the models with Memtrix memory layers, everything those layers know beyond the last 3 words has to come through their 64×64 memories, so a drop here is partly the memory at work. An attention model can look back at every word in its window, so for it this shows how much use it makes of a long context.

Later words get easier

Loss of words by where they sit in a text.
How to read this

The first words of a text are hard to guess: nothing has been said yet. As the text goes on, a model that remembers what it read can guess better. This shows the average loss for words 1–4, 4–16, … up to 256–1,024.

The honest weak spot of a fixed-size memory would show as a Memtrix-based line flattening or rising at the far right while the GPT keeps going down.

An attention model keeps every word of its window, so its line should keep going down to the far right. A line that flattens early means the model has not yet learned to use what lies far back.

Exact recall

A passage of 128 tokens, then the very same passage again: how much of the repeat does each model get exactly right?
Why this test?

The first time through, the passage is new; the second time, a model that really uses what it read gets almost every word. An attention model keeps every past word and can look back and copy, so for it this tests whether it has learned to copy (the "induction" trick transformers pick up early in training). It is the hardest test for a memory of fixed size: a Memtrix memory layer has to have stored the passage as it read.

Tide is built on Memtrix: underneath its belt of drafts it has the very same 12 blocks of 64×64 memories, written word by word by surprise. So this test measures Tide's memory just as it does Memtrix's. Its drafts could even help here: once Tide recognises the repeat, its drafts of the next few words can already be right.

What am I looking at?

Tide keeps a belt of drafts of the next 8 words. Every word of this text was drafted 8 times before it arrived: first 8 words ahead (a blurry guess), then 7 ahead, ..., then 1 ahead (the ordinary next-word guess). Inside Memtrix's memory

Reading a text with the lid off

Each word colored by how much it surprised the memories. Repeated facts at the end should glow less.
What am I looking at?

For every word, each memory first guesses what goes with it, then compares with the truth. The gap is the surprise, and the memory is fixed by that much (times its trust in the word). That is the whole learning rule.

Memory surprise is the size of that gap, averaged over all memories: bright = new, dark = "I knew that". Trust is how much each word was allowed to change the memory. Word loss is how unexpected the word was to the whole model.

How long each memory remembers

Half-life in words: after how many words half of what a memory holds has faded.
Why do they differ?

Every memory learns its own fade. Some forget within a few words (useful for grammar: "what was the last noun?"), some hold on for thousands (useful for "who is this text about?"). Each dot is one memory, one row per block. The scale is logarithmic.

What a memory looks like

One 64 × 64 memory after reading the text, in the first, middle and last block.
What do the colors mean?

Each square is one of the 4,096 numbers of the memory: orange positive, blue negative, dark near zero. Rows are the "situations" (keys), columns are the "answers" (values). A memory full of structure is one that is being written to; a blank one is not used.
