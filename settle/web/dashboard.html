What is loss?

Each time a model reads a word it guesses the next one, giving every possible word a chance. Loss measures how surprised it was by the word that really came next. Lower means it saw it coming.

A handy way to read it: a loss of L is as hard as picking the right word out of about eL equally likely words.

Solid lines are measured on held-out stories, so they show real skill. Faint lines are the training batches. Both models have the same number of parameters and see the same words in the same order, so it is a fair race.

What is a step?

When Settle reads a word, the word's column of cells keeps updating, step after step, until it stops changing, like a ripple fading out. Easy words calm down in a few steps; hard ones take longer.

The lens is a short second pass: the last few words are re-read together, in both directions, before Settle guesses the next word.

The lower chart counts words that hit the step cap, the maximum Settle is allowed. Fewer is better: it means the sheet learned to come to rest.

Inside Settle

The sheet

A photograph of Settle reading a test passage. One column per word, one row per distance.
How do I read the sheet?

Every word gets a column of cells. Each row reaches a different distance into the past: the bottom row looks one or two words back, the next looks twice as far, and so on, so the top row sees the big picture.

Colour shows what a cell is "thinking": cells with similar states get similar colours, so you can spot ideas that repeat.

Every few minutes the trainer feeds this passage in word by word, exactly like the chat app, and takes a photo. The bars under the sheet show how many steps each word needed. Watch it settle replays the training-time view, where all columns settle at the same time; a bright outline means the column is still moving.

Does writing match training?

Two ways of reading the same passage should agree.
Why does this matter?

During training, Settle reads a whole passage at once. When chatting, it adds one word at a time. Settle is built so both give the same answer; if they drifted apart, what it learned would not carry over to writing.

We check every photo: the largest difference in the model's scores (should stay near zero) and how often both pick the same top word (should be 100%).
